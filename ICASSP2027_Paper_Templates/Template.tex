\begin{abstract}
Fine-tuning improves speech recognition by changing an encoder's representation, but it is unclear which parts of that change the adapted recognizer actually uses. We study this question with a controlled intervention on a Wav2Vec2-Large-960h recognizer. We define the input-conditioned representation shift as the difference between fine-tuned and pretrained final-layer representations, estimate coordinate utility with a first-order Taylor criterion derived from the fine-tuned CTC loss, and intervene while keeping the original CTC readout fixed. On the official L2-ARCTIC UT8 Fold~0 split, the complete shift reaches $10.499\%$ test WER, whereas removing it from the adapted readout gives $118.912\%$. Retaining the top half of the $1024$ shift coordinates gives $11.713\%$, preserving $98.9\%$ of the adaptation gain. The result shows that the functional effect of fine-tuning is concentrated in a small subset of representation changes.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
ASR, SSL, CTC, selection

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a standard foundation for automatic speech recognition (ASR), with models such as wav2vec~2.0, HuBERT, and WavLM providing strong representations that can be adapted to downstream speech domains through fine-tuning~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec,hsu2021hubert,chen2022wavlm}. Fine-tuning, however, does more than improve endpoint recognition accuracy: it moves the speech representation away from its pretrained state. Recent work has shown that this difference, or \emph{Delta embedding}, itself contains useful task-specific information and can improve ASR when fused with other SSL representations~\cite{wang2026mind}. This raises a natural question: \emph{does the adapted recognizer rely on the entire fine-tuning-induced shift, or is its useful effect concentrated in only part of it?}

Prior representation analyses have shown that ASR fine-tuning substantially changes SSL representations, particularly in upper encoder layers~\cite{pasad2021layerwise}, while work on task vectors has demonstrated that differences between pretrained and fine-tuned models can encode adaptation information in parameter space~\cite{taskvectorasr2024}. Delta embedding fusion extends this observation to speech representations~\cite{wang2026mind}. However, these studies establish that fine-tuning-induced differences are informative, not which parts of those differences actually support the recognition decisions of the adapted model. In particular, shift magnitude alone need not indicate functional importance: a large representation change may have little influence on the CTC decision, while a smaller change may occur along a direction to which the recognizer is highly sensitive.

In this work, we introduce \textbf{Decision-Aligned Delta Selection (DADS)} to identify decision-relevant components of the fine-tuning shift. For an utterance $x$, we define the shift as $\Delta(x)=E_{\ft}(x)-E_{\pt}(x)$ and score each coordinate using a first-order Taylor criterion that combines its displacement with the sensitivity of the fine-tuned CTC loss. We then directly mask Delta coordinates while keeping the original fine-tuned CTC head and decoding procedure fixed. This frozen-head intervention prevents a new classifier from compensating for the removed shift and allows us to test the shift itself. We evaluate both retention, which asks how much of the shift is sufficient to preserve recognition, and deletion, which asks whether removing high- and low-utility coordinates produces different behavioral effects.

Experiments on the official L2-ARCTIC UT8 splits show that fine-tuning-induced shifts are highly non-uniform in their contribution to recognition. On Fold~0, the complete shift achieves $10.499\%$ WER, while retaining $75\%$ of the DADS-ranked coordinates gives $10.585\%$ and retaining $50\%$ gives $11.713\%$. At the same $50\%$ budget, magnitude selection reaches $16.638\%$. More strikingly, removing the lowest-utility quarter leaves WER at $10.585\%$, whereas removing the highest-utility quarter raises it to $131.190\%$. The same ordering is reproduced on two additional official folds. These results show that the representation changes introduced by fine-tuning are not equally important: substantial portions can be removed with little effect, while a smaller set of decision-aligned changes is critical to the adapted recognizer.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We study the internal structure of fine-tuning-induced representation shifts and ask which components are actually used by an adapted ASR model.
\item We propose DADS, a simple CTC-aware Taylor criterion evaluated through a frozen-head representation intervention, requiring no retraining after selection.
\item We show across official L2-ARCTIC folds that decision-relevant fine-tuning shifts are strongly concentrated: DADS preserves substantially more recognition performance than matched magnitude selection, while high- and low-utility deletion produces sharply different effects.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\noindent\textbf{Overview.}
We treat fine-tuning as a measurable intervention on representation space. For the same utterance, the pretrained and adapted encoders expose a paired shift; a binary gate then supplies selected coordinates of that shift to the already adapted readout. This turns functional support into a controlled counterfactual: the encoder, readout, decoding rule, and examples stay fixed while the supplied shift changes. Decision-Aligned Delta Selection (DADS) estimates the support with a one-shot Taylor utility and tests the estimate through complementary retention and deletion interventions. Figure~\ref{fig:formal-main} gives the complete measurement loop.

\subsection{Fine-tuning shifts as paired representation changes}
\label{sec:shift}

Let $f_{\pt}$ and $f_{\ft}$ denote pretrained and fine-tuned versions of the same encoder, evaluated on the same waveform and with the same preprocessing. For an utterance $x$, their final-layer representations are
\begin{equation}
E_{\pt}(x)=f_{\pt}(x),
\qquad
E_{\ft}(x)=f_{\ft}(x),
\end{equation}
where $E_{\pt}(x),E_{\ft}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{T_x\times d}$. Because both representations are obtained from the same encoder architecture and the same input, their difference defines an input-conditioned fine-tuning shift,
\begin{equation}
\Delta(x)=E_{\ft}(x)-E_{\pt}(x).
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}

Thus,
\begin{equation}
E_{\ft}(x)=E_{\pt}(x)+\Delta(x).
\label{eq:reconstruction}
\end{equation}

We study $\Delta$ in the encoder's native representation basis and preserve its frame axis. The unit of selection is therefore a coordinate of the representation shift, not a parameter, a source layer, or a learned latent feature.

\subsection{Frozen-readout interventions and functional support}
\label{sec:functional-support}

Let $g_{\ft}$ denote the CTC readout learned by the fine-tuned recognizer, and let $\operatorname{Dec}$ denote the fixed greedy CTC decoding rule. We intervene on the representation shift while leaving this adapted decision rule unchanged. For a binary coordinate mask $m\in\{0,1\}^{d}$, shared across time, define
\begin{equation}
\widetilde E_m(x)=E_{\pt}(x)+m\odot\Delta(x),
\label{eq:masked-representation}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is broadcast over frames. A one keeps a shift coordinate; a zero removes it. The encoder and CTC readout remain fixed, so masking is a counterfactual on the supplied shift, not retraining of a smaller recognizer.

The two endpoints have a direct interpretation:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde E_{\mathbf{1}}(x)=E_{\ft}(x),
\qquad
\widetilde E_{\mathbf{0}}(x)=E_{\pt}(x).
\end{equation}

The zero-mask endpoint is therefore not a pretrained-ASR baseline. It is the pretrained representation evaluated by the frozen fine-tuned readout: the representation change is removed, but the decision rule that learned to use it is preserved.

Let $[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}$, let $S\subseteq[d]$ denote a subset of retained coordinates, and define its mask by $m_S(i)=\mathbb{1}[i\in S]$. We define the functional support of the shift as the smallest coordinate subset that preserves the behavior of the adapted recognizer. For a behavioral tolerance $\epsilon$,
\begin{equation}
S_{\epsilon}^{\star}
=
\arg\min_{S\subseteq[d]} |S|
\quad
\text{s.t.}
\quad
\mathcal{B}(m_S)
\le
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{1})+\epsilon,
\label{eq:functional-support}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}(m)$ denotes recognition error under $\operatorname{Dec}(g_{\ft}(\widetilde E_m))$ on a fixed evaluation set. The $2^d$ possible masks make exhaustive search impractical; DADS supplies a one-shot ordering whose sufficiency and necessity can be tested directly.

\subsection{Decision-Aligned Delta Selection}
\label{sec:dads}

We estimate coordinate importance from the local sensitivity of the actual fine-tuned CTC objective rather than from shift magnitude alone. For an utterance--transcript pair $(x,y)$, introduce a continuous coordinate gate $\alpha\in[0,1]^d$ and define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{x,y}(\alpha)
=
\mathcal{L}_{\CTC}
\left(
g_{\ft}
\left(
E_{\pt}(x)+\alpha\odot\Delta(x)
\right),
y
\right).
\label{eq:gated-loss}
\end{equation}

The full fine-tuned representation corresponds to $\alpha=\mathbf{1}$. The sensitivity of the loss to coordinate $i$ at this endpoint is
\begin{equation}
\left.
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{x,y}}
{\partial\alpha_i}
\right|_{\alpha=\mathbf{1}}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T_x}
\Delta_{t,i}(x)
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{x,y}}
{\partial\Delta_{t,i}(x)}.
\label{eq:global-gate-gradient}
\end{equation}

A first-order removal therefore depends jointly on how much coordinate $i$ changed during fine-tuning and how sensitive the adapted CTC objective is to that change. Magnitude alone captures only the former. For each frame and coordinate, define the local decision-aligned contribution
\begin{equation}
q_{t,i}(x,y)
=
\Delta_{t,i}(x)
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{x,y}}
{\partial\Delta_{t,i}(x)}.
\label{eq:local-contribution}
\end{equation}

The global derivative in Eq.~\ref{eq:global-gate-gradient} can cancel across frames. DADS instead uses a non-cancelling framewise aggregation, preserving the local evidence that the CTC objective assigns to each encoder frame. Given a fixed utility-estimation subset $\mathcal{D}_{u}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}$, we define
\begin{equation}
U_i
=
\frac{1}{N_u}
\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{u}}
\sum_{t=1}^{T_x}
\left|
q_{t,i}(x,y)
\right|,
\qquad
N_u=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{u}}T_x.
\label{eq:dads}
\end{equation}

This non-cancelling aggregation is related to the global coordinate-gate sensitivity through
\begin{equation}
\left|
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{x,y}}
{\partial\alpha_i}
\right|
=
\left|\sum_t q_{t,i}\right|
\le
\sum_t |q_{t,i}|.
\end{equation}

Thus, $U_i$ is a first-order, decision-aligned saliency: it combines the size of the learned shift with the sensitivity of the true CTC objective to that shift. In implementation, the per-utterance CTC loss is normalized by transcript length before differentiation and padded frames are excluded. For the magnitude baseline, coordinates are ranked by the same frame-normalized aggregation of $|\Delta_{t,i}|$; random masks are sampled at the same retained budget.

The ranking is computed once from $\mathcal{D}_{u}$. No encoder parameters, CTC parameters, decoder parameters, or ranking scores are updated after coordinate selection.

\subsection{Retention and deletion interventions}
\label{sec:interventions}

Let $\pi$ denote the ordering of coordinates induced by $U_i$. We use the same ranking for two complementary interventions.

\noindent\textbf{Retention: testing sufficiency.}
For a budget $K$, let
\begin{equation}
S_K^{\mathrm{keep}}=\{\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_K\},
\end{equation}
and evaluate
\begin{equation}
g_{\ft}
\left(
E_{\pt}(x)
+m_{S_K^{\mathrm{keep}}}\odot\Delta(x)
\right).
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
This is the sufficiency test: are the highest-utility coordinates enough to recover the behavior induced by the complete shift?

\noindent\textbf{Deletion: testing necessity.}
We additionally begin from the complete shift and remove either the highest- or lowest-utility coordinates. For a deletion budget $K$,
\begin{align}
S_K^{\mathrm{drop\text{-}high}}
&=[d]\setminus\{\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_K\},\\
S_K^{\mathrm{drop\text{-}low}}
&=[d]\setminus\{\pi_{d-K+1},\ldots,\pi_d\}.
\end{align}

This is the necessity test: are the coordinates identified by DADS more necessary to the adapted decision rule than coordinates ranked as low utility?

All interventions use the same frozen CTC readout and the same decoding procedure. Consequently, any behavioral difference between masks is produced by the supplied representation shift rather than by downstream retraining or recovery.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the results around three questions: how much of the shift is sufficient, which coordinates are necessary, and whether the pattern is reproduced across folds.

\subsection{How much of the shift is sufficient?}

We first compare the complete shift, the no-shift counterfactual, and matched-budget coordinate selection on official UT8 Fold~0. Table~\ref{tab:retention} shows that retaining only 512 of the 1024 shift coordinates gives $11.713\%$ WER and preserves $98.9\%$ of the full adaptation gain. At the same budget, magnitude selection gives $16.638\%$. Retaining 75\% of the coordinates gives $10.585\%$, close to the full-shift result of $10.499\%$.

\midrule

The large gap between the no-shift and full-shift endpoints establishes that the adapted readout uses the fine-tuning change. The near-full performance of the retained masks establishes a separate result: a substantial part of that change is functionally redundant under the frozen decision rule. This is a behavioral redundancy result, not a claim that half of the encoder can be removed computationally.

\subsection{Which coordinates are necessary?}

Retention alone tests sufficiency. To test necessity, we start from the complete shift and remove either the highest- or lowest-utility quarter. Removing the highest-utility coordinates raises WER to $131.190\%$, whereas removing the lowest-utility coordinates gives $10.585\%$. The same ranking that finds a sufficient retained subset therefore distinguishes behaviorally critical coordinates from coordinates that can be removed with little effect. The matched Magnitude mask at $K=512$ gives $16.638\%$ WER, compared with $11.713\%$ for DADS, showing why shift size alone is not an adequate selection criterion.

\subsection{Does the finding transfer across folds?}

We repeat the matched 50\% comparison with independently trained replicas on two additional official folds. DADS reaches $16.011\%$ and $15.579\%$ WER on Folds~1 and~2, compared with $16.628\%$ and $16.485\%$ for magnitude selection. Dropping the top utility quarter is also consistently more damaging than dropping the bottom quarter. The ordering therefore does not depend on the single Fold~0 checkpoint, although the present evidence remains tied to this encoder family, representation basis, and frozen-readout protocol.
